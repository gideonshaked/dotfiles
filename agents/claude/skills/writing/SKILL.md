# Writing

Write with these rules from the first sentence, not as a pass over a finished draft.

## The six rules

George Orwell set out six rules in 'Politics and the English Language' (1946).

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

The rules serve clarity, so break one when following it would make a sentence clumsy or change its meaning.
Rule 6 is also what licenses keeping a word that a list below bans when that word is the right technical term.

## Four habits to hunt

Orwell names four habits that let a writer assemble sentences from ready-made parts without checking what they mean.

- Dying metaphors. Images used so often that the writer no longer sees the picture, such as move the needle, low-hanging fruit, deep dive, level playing field. Drop the image and say the thing plainly.
- Operators. A plain verb swapped for a phrase built round a noun, which pads the sentence and hides who does what. Write "cause" for "give rise to", "tends" for "exhibits a tendency to", "use" for "make use of". The same habit produces padded connectives such as "with respect to", "the fact that" and "in order to".
- Pretentious diction. Words that dress up a plain statement, such as utilize, leverage, facilitate, delve, robust, seamless, comprehensive.
- Meaningless words. Words so loose they carry no agreed content, such as crucial, key, significant outside statistics, genuine, real, actual, honest. Cut them, or say what specifically makes the thing matter.

## Punctuation

Three marks are banned outright, and rule 6 does not license breaking these.

- No em dashes. Write two sentences, or use a comma.
- No semicolons. Write two sentences.
- No colons, except to introduce a list.

A colon before a list is the only permitted use.
Not before an explanation, an example or a definition, and not for emphasis.
Each of these marks bolts a second clause onto a finished thought, and a full stop makes the writer decide what that clause is.

## Shape

A draft with clean diction and no banned marks can still read as machine-written.
The tell is usually that every sentence has the same shape.

- Vary sentence length. Model prose collapses into a band of roughly 12 to 26 words and stays there. Write some sentences of eight words or fewer. Let others run long.
- Cut resultative tails. These are the comma-plus-participle endings bolted onto a finished sentence, such as ", making it easier to scale" or ", ensuring consistency". Stop at the finished sentence, or make the consequence its own sentence.
- Unstage clefts. "What actually mattered was the cost." "It is the timing that breaks this." Both delay the subject to build suspense the sentence has not earned. Write "The cost mattered."
- Kill copula-only paragraphs. A paragraph where every verb is a form of "to be" describes a state and reports no event. Find the thing that happens and put it in a verb.
- Vary paragraph length too. Uniform paragraphs are the same tell one level up.
- Put the verdict first. Model prose builds to its conclusion, so the reader meets the reasoning before the point. Lead with what you concluded, then say why.
- Keep concessions short and lopsided. Giving each side equal space reads as a refusal to decide.

## Shorthand

Working notes leak habits into prose that a reader cannot decode.

- No arrow chains such as "parse -> validate -> store". Write the sentence that says what happens.
- No stacked hyphen compounds such as "per-session token-cache fix". Unpack them into a phrase.
- Introduce a label before you use it. A name coined while working, such as "option B" or "the phase 2 fix", is fine once the text has said plainly what it names, but never drop one in as if the reader already knew it.

Before handing a draft back, reread it once against each section above.
